**Scope.** This chapter gives the working definition of a humanoid used in this library, how a humanoid body is described in degrees of freedom, what legs and a human-like body plan buy and cost, and the main options for hands. It does not compare commercial platforms; that is Chapter 7.1 (Planned). Numbers in worked examples are **illustrative estimates** unless a source sits next to them.

## 1.2.1 A Working Definition

In this library the following definition will be used for a humanoid robot:

> A **humanoid robot** is a mobile robot whose body plan approximates a person's: a torso carrying two arms with end effectors, sensing mounted high on the body, and locomotion by two legs or, in some designs, a wheeled base. It is sized and shaped to work in spaces, at workstations, and with objects designed for people.

The definition is functional, not cosmetic. A face, a head shape, or a human-like skin does not make a robot a humanoid in this library; a body that can reach, carry, and move where a person would is what matters. The second sentence is the reason anyone builds one ([[1.3 - Why Build a Humanoid|Chapter 1.3]]).

## 1.2.2 Degrees of Freedom and Body Plan

A **degree of freedom** (DoF) is one independent way a body can move. For a robot it usually means one actuated joint: a revolute joint rotates about one axis, so it adds one DoF.

**Why six matters.** Placing a rigid object, such as a hand or a foot, at an arbitrary position and orientation in 3D space takes six numbers: three for position and three for orientation. A limb therefore needs at least six joints to reach arbitrary poses within its workspace. Arms often have seven, giving one extra (*redundant*) DoF that lets the elbow move while the hand stays still, useful for avoiding obstacles and joint limits.

**A typical layout** (illustrative, not a specific robot):

| Body segment | Joints | Typical arrangement |
|---|---|---|
| Each leg | 6 | Hip 3, knee 1, ankle 2 |
| Each arm | 7 | Shoulder 3, elbow 1, wrist 3 |
| Torso | 1 | Waist yaw |
| Head or neck | 2 | Pan and tilt for cameras |
| Each hand | 1 (gripper) to 20+ (dexterous hand) | Parallel gripper: 1 |

With two 1-DoF grippers that is $2 \times 6 + 2 \times 7 + 1 + 2 + 2 = 31$ actuated joints.

**The floating base.** A fixed industrial arm is bolted to the floor, so its joint angles fully describe its pose. A humanoid's pelvis is not attached to anything. Its full configuration needs the pelvis position and orientation (six numbers) as well as the $n$ joint angles:

$$
q = \begin{bmatrix} q_{\text{base}} \\ q_{\text{joints}} \end{bmatrix}, \qquad q_{\text{base}} \in \mathbb{R}^{6}, \quad q_{\text{joints}} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}
$$

where $q$ is the robot's full configuration: every number needed to say exactly where each part of the body is (strictly, an orientation is not an ordinary three-number vector, but six numbers are enough for counting). For the layout above, $6 + 31 = 37$ configuration variables, but only 31 motors. The six base variables have no motor of their own: the robot moves its pelvis only by pushing on the world through its feet or hands. This is what **underactuated** means here, and it is why contact forces and balance ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.4) sit at the centre of humanoid control (Chapter 3.7, Planned).

**Real robots vary widely.** Two research platforms already cited in this library:

- **iCub** (research humanoid): "104 cm tall" with "53 degrees of freedom": 6 in the head, 16 in each arm, 3 in the torso, and 6 in each leg ([Nori et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.3389/frobt.2015.00006); see [[Source Note - Nori 2015 iCub Whole-Body Control]]). The 16 per arm include the hand.
- **Berkeley Humanoid Lite** (open-source research humanoid): weighs "16 kg and stands 0.8 m tall", with "two 5-DOF arms and integrated grippers"; joints are driven by brushless motors through 3D-printed cycloidal gearboxes ([Chi et al. 2025](https://doi.org/10.15607/RSS.2025.XXI.062); see [[Source Note - Chi 2025 Berkeley Humanoid Lite]]).

Five-DoF arms cannot reach every orientation; they trade reach generality for lower cost and mass, which is a reasonable choice when the tasks are known. Every joint added brings mass, cost, heat, and another part that can fail, which is why designers remove joints a task does not need (reliability is covered in Chapter 5.4, Planned).

## 1.2.3 Legs or Wheels: What the Body Plan Buys and Costs

### What a human-like body buys

- **Access.** Steps, thresholds, ramps, cluttered floors, narrow aisles, and stairs, all built for people.
- **Reach range.** Squatting or kneeling to the floor and reaching overhead, so one robot can handle objects on the floor and on high shelves.
- **Existing workstations and tools.** Benches, carts, handles, and fixtures designed for a person's height and reach.
- **Redeployment.** In principle, moving between tasks without rebuilding the site. This is the central commercial claim, and it is tested, not assumed, in Parts 4, 6, and 7.

### What it costs

- **Active balance.** A biped is *dynamically* stable: its centre of mass must be actively kept over a small support area, so it spends power just standing and can fall if control or power fails. A wheeled base with a wide footprint is *statically* stable: it stays upright with motors off, which makes stopping safely much simpler ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.5; Chapter 5.3, Planned).
- **More actuators.** Legs add at least 12 joints, each with its mass, cost, heat, and failure exposure.
- **Locomotion energy.** Walking generally costs more energy per metre than rolling on a smooth floor, by an amount that depends heavily on the walker's design.

### Cost of transport

The standard way to compare locomotion efficiency across machines of different size is the dimensionless **specific cost of transport**: energy used per unit weight per unit distance.

$$
c = \frac{E}{m g d} = \frac{P}{m g v}
$$

where $E$ is energy used over distance $d$, $m$ is mass, $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, $P$ is average power, and $v$ is speed. The *energetic* cost of transport $c_{\text{et}}$ uses total electrical energy drawn; the *mechanical* cost $c_{\text{mt}}$ uses only positive mechanical work at the joints. Lower is better.

### Worked example: walking energy in the tote cycle (illustrative estimate)

Assume the robot plus tote is $m = 70\ \text{kg}$ and each transfer cycle walks $d = 20\ \text{m}$ (10 m out, 10 m back). Walking energy per cycle is $E = c\, m g d$. The three values of $c$ are assumptions chosen to span a range, not measurements: an efficient walker, an inefficient walker, and a wheeled base on a smooth floor.

$$
\begin{aligned}
c = 0.5: &\quad E = 0.5 \times 70\ \text{kg} \times 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2 \times 20\ \text{m} = 6{,}867\ \text{J} = 1.9\ \text{Wh} \\
c = 2: &\quad E = 27{,}468\ \text{J} = 7.6\ \text{Wh} \\
c = 0.01: &\quad E = 137\ \text{J} = 0.04\ \text{Wh}
\end{aligned}
$$

For scale, assume the robot draws a baseline of 300 W just to stay on and upright (illustrative): over a 60 s cycle that is $300\ 	ext{W} 	imes 60\ 	ext{s} = 5.0\ 	ext{Wh}$. Lifting a 10 kg tote 0.6 m takes only about 59 J of mechanical work, under 0.05 Wh from the battery even after losses. Depending on the walker, walking can cost as much as the standing baseline and far more than the lift, while a wheeled base makes it almost negligible. Measured cost of transport for current humanoids carrying payloads is not established in this library yet.

## 1.2.4 Hands and End Effectors

The hand is where the body plan meets the task, and the choice spans a wide range:

- **Parallel or task-specific grippers.** One or two actuators, robust, cheap, and easy to control; limited to objects they were designed for.
- **Swappable tools.** A tool changer lets one robot carry different grippers for different tasks. Agility describes Digit 5's "swappable gripper design" ([Agility, Digit 5 page](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/solutions/digit-5); vendor-reported, product "in development"; see [[Source Note - Agility Robotics Digit 5 Product Page]]).
- **Multi-fingered dexterous hands.** Many actuators, able to handle varied objects and human tools, but harder to build, control, protect, and keep calibrated.

Chapter [[2.7 - Hands Grippers and End Effectors|2.7]] treats hands in depth.

## 1.2.5 Implications for the Two Running Cases

- **Tote transfer.** On a flat warehouse floor, a wheeled base with a humanoid upper body, or a single-arm mobile manipulator, can likely reach the same conveyors and shelves with less energy, fewer actuators, and simpler safe stopping. Legs earn their cost where the route has steps, thresholds, or clutter, or where totes sit on the floor and must be picked from a squat. A simple gripper can handle a tote. Whether a site has those features is a requirement question for [[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]].
- **Fixture loading.** Body-shop floors are flat and stations are fixed, so the simpler alternative is a fixed industrial robot cell. A humanoid's case rests on fitting into a station laid out for a person, without guarding and re-layout, and on moving between stations. Thin, sharp, flexible sheet metal needs a more careful choice of gripper than a tote. BMW reports its Figure 02 pilot covered about 1.2 million steps in about 1,250 operating hours ([BMW Group, 2026-02-27](https://newsroom.bmwgroup.com/global/bmw-group-to-deploy-humanoid-robots-in-production-in-germany-for-the-first-time); customer-reported), so the robot walked as part of the job; the release does not say whether a wheeled or fixed design was considered.
