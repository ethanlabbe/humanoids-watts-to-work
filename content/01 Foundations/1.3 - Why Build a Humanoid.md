**Scope.** This chapter sets out the case for building robots in human form, the case against, a simple cost model for when a general-purpose robot beats special-purpose automation, the conditions under which simpler options win, and the claims that still need evidence. It argues from engineering and cost structure, not from market forecasts. Numbers in worked examples are **illustrative estimates**.

## 1.3.1 The Case for the Human Form

The argument has three parts. Each is a hypothesis to test, not a settled fact.

**1. The world is already built for people.** Doorways, aisles, stairs, shelf heights, workbenches, carts, handles, and tools are all sized for the human body. A robot with the same reach, footprint, and hands can, in principle, work in an existing site without changing it. Rebuilding a site for conventional automation (guarding, conveyors, fixtures, re-layout) can cost more than the automation itself, and many sites cannot stop production to do it. Vendors make this argument directly: Agility states that "Digit's human form factor means industrial environments don't have to be redesigned" ([Agility, solutions page](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/solution); vendor claim; see [[Source Note - Agility Robotics Solutions Page]]).

**2. One machine, many tasks.** Special-purpose automation does one job very well and nothing else. A general-purpose body could move between tasks as demand changes, so its cost is shared across all of them, and adding a task becomes a software and integration job rather than a new machine. This is the economic core of the case and is modelled in §1.3.3 below.

**3. Human-shaped data and know-how.** If a robot's body resembles a person's, human demonstrations, teleoperation, and existing job instructions map onto it more directly, which may make new tasks faster to teach (Chapters 3.10 and 3.11, Planned). How much this helps in practice is not established in this library.

Customers frame the case more narrowly. BMW, after its pilot at Spartanburg, describes humanoids as "a value‑adding complement to existing automation", with "potential in monotonous, ergonomically demanding, or safety‑critical tasks", and states the goal "to relieve employees and further improve working conditions" ([BMW Group, 2026-02-27](https://newsroom.bmwgroup.com/global/bmw-group-to-deploy-humanoid-robots-in-production-in-germany-for-the-first-time); customer statement; see [[Source Note - BMW Group 2026 Humanoid Robots in Production]]). That is a claim about filling gaps that existing automation leaves, not about replacing it.

## 1.3.2 The Case Against

- **Specialists outperform generalists at their one job.** A conveyor, a sorter, or a fixed robot cell is typically faster, more reliable, and cheaper per operation than a general-purpose machine doing the same narrow task. Where one task runs at high, steady volume, specialization usually wins.
- **Generality only pays if it is used.** A robot that could do ten tasks but is deployed on one carries the cost of its unused capability: extra joints, sensors, compute, and software.
- **The body plan has running costs.** Active balance, more actuators, higher locomotion energy, and harder safe stopping ([[1.2 - What Is a Humanoid Robot|1.2]] §1.2.3) are paid on every shift, whether or not the task needs legs.
- **Sites can change.** Many layouts are rebuilt anyway, on refurbishment or for new product lines. When a site is being changed regardless, the "no redesign" advantage shrinks.
- **The human form is not optimal for every task.** Two hands, a fixed height, and a limited payload are constraints as well as conveniences. Many tasks are easier for a machine with more arms, longer reach, or no need to balance.
- **Safety near people is harder.** A strong, fast, mobile machine with a high center of mass working beside people needs safety functions that are still maturing; Agility itself notes for Digit 5 that "Certain safety features are still in development" ([Agility, Digit 5 page](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/solutions/digit-5); vendor statement; see [[Source Note - Agility Robotics Digit 5 Product Page]]). Chapter 5.3 (Planned) covers safety.

## 1.3.3 Generality versus Specialization: A Simple Cost Model

The case for a general-purpose robot rests on sharing its cost across tasks that would each otherwise need their own machine. A simple model shows when that works.

Let $A_d$ be the annualized cost of one dedicated system (purchase spread over its life, plus integration, energy, and maintenance per year), and $A_h$ the annualized cost of one humanoid. Suppose there are $k$ tasks, each low-volume enough that one humanoid could cover all of them by moving between them, while dedicated automation would need one system per task. The humanoid is cheaper when

$$
A_h < k\, A_d \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad k > \frac{A_h}{A_d}
$$

That is only true if the humanoid has the time to do all $k$ tasks. If task $i$ occurs $V_i$ times a year, takes $t_i$ hours each time, and switching to it costs $s_i$ hours (walking, tool change, set-up), the humanoid's available hours $H$ per year must cover

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{k} V_i\,(t_i + s_i) \le H
$$

And what matters to the customer is cost per *successful* task. With success rate $p$ (the fraction of attempts completed without human intervention):

$$
C_{\text{task}} = \frac{A_h}{p \sum_{i=1}^{k} V_i}
$$

### Worked example (illustrative estimate)

Assume a humanoid costs three times as much per year as one dedicated system, $A_h = 3 A_d$. Then:

- It needs to replace **more than three** dedicated systems ($k > 3$) to be cheaper, and only if it has the hours to do all of them.
- If switching overhead uses a third of its hours, the number of tasks it can cover shrinks by about a third, which can leave it short of the four it needs to break even.
- If its success rate is $p = 0.9$ and the dedicated systems' is close to 1, each successful task costs about 11% more than the formula with $p = 1$ suggests ($1/0.9 \approx 1.11$), before counting the cost of the interventions themselves.

The model leaves out a lot: dedicated systems can also be shared, people are a third option, and integration costs differ by site. It still shows the shape of the case. A humanoid needs **several low-volume tasks, close together, with short switching times and high success rates**. One high-volume task is the territory of dedicated automation. Chapters 6.3 and 6.4 (Planned) build this into a full cost model with measured inputs.

## 1.3.4 When Simpler Options Win

Most jobs a humanoid could do have an established alternative. The comparison below is qualitative and general; specific sites need specific numbers.

| Option | Best at | Weak at | Site changes needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Person | Variety, judgement, handling the unexpected | Repetitive, heavy, or hazardous work; labour cost and availability | None |
| Conveyor or sorter | High, steady volume along a fixed path | Changes in layout or product; anything off the path | Large, permanent |
| Autonomous mobile robot (AMR) | Moving loads between fixed points on flat floors | Picking up or manipulating objects | Small (floor, charging, sometimes markers) |
| Mobile manipulator (wheeled base with an arm) | Moving and simple handling on flat floors | Steps, clutter, reaching low and high | Small to moderate |
| Fixed robot cell | Fast, precise, repetitive work at one station | Anything outside the cell; changeover | Moderate to large (guarding, fixtures, re-layout) |
| Humanoid | Several varied tasks in a space built for people | Speed and cost per operation on any single task; maturity | Small, in principle |

Simpler options usually win when:

- one task dominates and its volume is high and steady;
- the floor is flat and the route is fixed;
- the site is being redesigned anyway;
- the task needs speed, precision, or payload beyond a human-sized body.

A humanoid has a case when:

- several different, low-volume tasks sit close together;
- the layout is built for people and cannot easily change;
- tasks change often, so dedicated equipment would be idle or obsolete;
- the route includes steps, thresholds, or clutter that wheeled machines cannot handle.

## 1.3.5 Claims That Need Evidence

The case for humanoids rests on claims that can be tested. Here is what each would take to establish, and what the library's sources show so far.

- **"No site redesign needed."** *Evidence needed:* integration hours and site modifications recorded at real deployments. *So far:* stated by a vendor (Agility, above); no deployment in the [[Deployment Tracker]] discloses integration effort.
- **"Safe beside people."** *Evidence needed:* risk assessments, safety-function certification, and incident records over many operating hours. *So far:* BMW reports its pilot "confirmed that humanoid robots can safely perform precise, repetitive work steps" (customer-reported, details not disclosed); Agility says some Digit 5 safety features are still in development (vendor).
- **"One robot, many tasks."** *Evidence needed:* the same robot doing several different tasks in paid operation, with switching time and success rate per task. *So far:* Agility describes Digit at GXO moving items "on and off an AMR to a conveyor" and "stacking totes onto a different floor location" ([Agility, 2025-11-20](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/content/digit-moves-over-100k-totes); vendor-reported; see [[Source Note - Agility Robotics 2025 Digit 100000 Totes]]), two related handling tasks, without per-task figures.
- **"Cheaper per task than the alternative."** *Evidence needed:* cost per successful task for the humanoid and the alternative at the same site. *So far:* no cited source discloses cost per task.
- **"Quick to deploy and teach."** *Evidence needed:* time from contract to productive operation, and time to add a new task. *So far:* BMW reports that "the transition from the laboratory to the actual production environment was faster than expected" (customer-reported, no durations given).

## 1.3.6 Implications for the Two Running Cases

- **Tote transfer.** Conveyors and AMRs are strong, mature alternatives on flat warehouse floors, so a humanoid's case rests on layouts that cannot change and on doing several kinds of handling with one machine. The documented GXO work (moving totes between AMRs, conveyors, and floor stacks) is the kind of multi-step handling where that case could hold, but no cost or per-task data is public.
- **Fixture loading.** Fixed robot cells are the standard way to load welding fixtures at high volume. A humanoid's case rests on stations laid out for people, lower-volume or frequently changing parts, and the ergonomic and safety-critical work BMW names as its motivation. BMW's own framing, a "complement to existing automation", fits this: humanoids for the gaps, cells for the volume.

## Open Questions

1. **Can a humanoid reach a cost per successful task that competes with people and with dedicated automation?** The cost model in §1.3.3 shows what that takes; no public data yet shows it achieved (§1.3.5).
2. **Can a humanoid be taught a new task quickly and cheaply enough for generality to pay off?** The case for one machine doing many tasks (§1.3.1) depends on it.
