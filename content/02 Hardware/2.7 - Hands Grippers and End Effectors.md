**Scope.** This chapter covers the part of the robot that touches the task. Topics:

- the grasp types, and the three ways a grasp holds an object;
- grip force and slip;
- the hardware options, from simple grippers to dexterous hands and tool changers;
- touch sensing and durability.

It is one of the chapters that also uses the fixture-loading contrast case. Grasp planning and contact-rich control software are in 3.8 (Planned). Numbers in worked examples are **illustrative estimates** unless a source sits next to them.

## 2.7.1 Purpose and Interfaces

The end effector is where the robot's work actually happens. Everything upstream, from the battery to the arm, exists to put it in the right place with the right force. [[1.2 - What Is a Humanoid Robot|1.2]] §1.2.4 sets out the range of choices; this chapter sizes them.

- **Energy:** power for the hand's own actuators, or for a vacuum pump; grip force out.
- **Data:** finger positions, grip force, touch sensing, and for suction the vacuum level.
- **Commands and feedback:** open, close, a force or position target in; whether an object is held, and whether it is slipping, out.
- **Timing:** slip has to be caught and corrected before the object is lost, and the faster the load is moving, the less time that leaves.
- **Physical:** the wrist mount, the hand's mass and the tool-changer interface. The hand sits at the end of the arm, so its mass loads every arm joint. An illustrative 1 kg hand at 0.5 m from the shoulder adds $1 \times 9.81 \times 0.5 = 4.9\ \text{N\,m}$, 14% on top of the 34.3 N m of holding the tote there ([[2.4 - Motors and Actuator Selection|2.4]] §2.4.4).

## 2.7.2 Fundamentals

### Grasp types

People hold things in a few dozen distinct ways. Feix et al. compared earlier classifications and arrived at 33 grasp types, which "can be reduced to a set of 17 more prototypical grasps" ([Feix et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.1109/THMS.2015.2470657)). Two ideas organize them, and both are useful for robot hands.

- **Power, intermediate and precision.** In a power grasp the object is held rigidly against the palm, so "all movements of the object have to be evoked by the arm". In a precision grasp the fingertips hold it and the hand can move it without moving the arm. Intermediate grasps mix the two.
- **Opposition.** The hand can squeeze an object in three basic directions: between the fingertips (pad opposition), against the palm (palm opposition), or from the side, as when holding a key (side opposition).

A tote carried by its handles is a power task. Picking a screw from a bin is a precision task. A hand that can do both needs many independent joints; a gripper built for one can be far simpler.

### How a grasp holds

There are three physical ways to hold an object ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.4):

- **Friction.** The hand squeezes, and friction between pads and object carries the load. The squeeze needed rises as friction falls, so it depends on the object's surface: dust, moisture or oil can lower friction sharply.
- **Form closure.** The hand hooks under or around the object, so geometry carries the load and friction is not needed. A hook under a tote's lip is the simplest example.
- **Suction.** A cup sealed on a surface holds by the pressure difference across it. It needs a smooth, closed surface and a vacuum source, and on a vertical surface it still relies on friction at the cup's rim.

### Grip force and slip

For a friction grasp between two pads, the object slips when the load along the surface exceeds what friction can carry. With a squeeze force $N$ on each side and friction coefficient $\mu$, two faces carry $2 \mu N$. The load is not only weight: accelerating the object upward adds to it. So the squeeze must satisfy

$$
2 \mu N \ge S\, m (g + a)
$$

where $m$ is the mass in kg, $g = 9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$, $a$ the upward acceleration in m/s², and $S$ a safety factor. Squeezing far harder than needed wastes power and can crush the object, so a good hand measures slip and adjusts. That is one of the main jobs of touch sensing.

### Actuation

- **Fully actuated:** one motor per joint. Every joint can be placed and force-controlled, at the cost of many motors in a small space.
- **Underactuated:** fewer motors than joints, with springs or differentials sharing the motion. The fingers wrap around an object's shape on their own. The Yale OpenHand project notes that underactuated hands "improve the generality of simple grippers by adaptively conforming to the surface of objects without the explicit need for sensors or complicated feedback systems" ([Yale OpenHand](https://www.eng.yale.edu/grablab/openhand/index.html)).
- **Tendon-driven:** motors sit in the forearm and pull cables through the wrist, keeping the hand itself light. The cost is cable stretch, friction and wear.

The transmission matters here as much as in the larger joints ([[2.5 - Transmissions and Joint Modules|2.5]] §2.5.2). A backdrivable finger can feel contact through its own motor current; a high-ratio finger cannot.

### Touch sensing

Touch sensors in the fingertips and palm report where the object is touching, how hard, and, for some sensors, whether it is starting to slide. They catch what cameras miss: small, transparent or hidden objects, and the first moment of slip. Several technologies are in use, including resistive, capacitive, magnetic and camera-based sensors ([Luo et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11261)). Their weakness is the soft skin they need, which wears.

### Durability

Hands touch everything, so they wear first. Pads abrade, tendons stretch and fray, skins tear, and small finger joints take the impacts of every bump. A hand's life is measured in grasp cycles, and every test has to state how many (§2.7.5).

## 2.7.3 Design Alternatives and Tradeoffs

### The options

| Option | Object range | Payload | Reliability | Cost | Control complexity |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Parallel-jaw gripper | Objects with two parallel faces | High for its size | High: one or two actuators | Low | Low |
| Suction | Smooth, sealed surfaces | Set by cup area and vacuum | High, but cups wear and leak | Low to moderate, plus a pump | Low |
| Underactuated hand | Many shapes, wrapped passively | Moderate | High: few actuators | Moderate | Low to moderate |
| Dexterous multi-fingered hand | Widest, including human tools | Moderate to high | Lower: many actuators and sensors | High | High |
| Task-specific tool, such as a tote hook | One object family | High | Very high | Low | Very low |
| Tool changer | Whatever tools are carried | Per tool | Adds a coupling that can fail | Moderate | Moderate |

The table is this library's summary. The dexterous hand row is where most humanoid makers are investing, and it is also where the trade is sharpest. Boston Dynamics describes hand design as "a ruthless tradeoff" between dexterity, strength, ruggedness, cost, repairability and sensing, and concludes that "a truly general purpose hand is not really an achievable goal" ([Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/robot-hands-for-modern-ai-and-real-work/)). Its own hand omits a fifth finger because that would mean "three more actuators, with the corresponding extra cost, volume, and probability of something breaking".

### Simpler alternative

A fixed industrial robot uses end-of-arm tooling built for one part: a set of suction cups, clamps or magnets laid out to match the part exactly, often with a tool changer to swap between parts. It does not need a general hand because the parts arrive in known positions. The humanoid's case for a dexterous hand rests on parts that do not arrive that way (§2.7.8).

## 2.7.4 Sizing or Worked Example

All values are **illustrative estimates** unless marked otherwise.

### Three ways to hold the 10 kg tote

The tote is shared between two hands, so each carries 5 kg. Lifting with an upward acceleration of 2 m/s² and a safety factor of 2, each hand must hold

$$
m (g + a) = 5 \times (9.81 + 2) = 59\ \text{N}
$$

with $S \times 59 = 118\ \text{N}$ to be held against.

**Friction pinch** on the tote wall, between two pads, from §2.7.2:

$$
N \ge \frac{S\, m (g + a)}{2 \mu} = \frac{118}{2 \times 0.5} = 118\ \text{N}
$$

with an illustrative pad-to-plastic friction coefficient of 0.5. If the tote is dusty or damp and $\mu$ falls to 0.3, the squeeze rises to 197 N.

**Hook under the lip.** Geometry carries the load, so the hook needs only to support the 59 N, or 118 N with the safety factor, with no squeeze at all. Friction does not enter.

**Suction on the tote wall.** On a vertical wall the cup holds by friction at its rim, so the vacuum force must be $118 / 0.5 = 236\ \text{N}$. At an illustrative vacuum of 60 kPa below atmospheric, that needs a cup area of $236 / 60{,}000 = 0.0039\ \text{m}^2$, a single cup about 71 mm across (87 mm at 40 kPa, 61 mm at 80 kPa).

| Method | Force the hand must make | Sensitive to | Robust to tote variation | Cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Friction pinch | 118 N squeeze, 197 N if friction drops | Surface friction, wall flex | Fair: works on any tote wall it can reach | Low |
| Hook under the lip | None beyond supporting 59 N | Lip shape and position | Good for totes with a lip; useless without one | Lowest |
| Suction on the wall | 236 N of vacuum force, a 71 mm cup | Ribs, labels, damage that break the seal | Poor on ribbed walls | Moderate, plus a pump |

**What it shows.** The hook is the most robust for a lipped tote: it does not depend on friction, needs no squeeze, and fails only if the tote has no lip. A friction pinch has to squeeze twice the tote's share of weight even in good conditions, and the squeeze depends on a friction coefficient the robot cannot see. Suction needs the most force and a smooth wall, which many totes, with ribs and labels, do not have.

### Tote hook: the gripper for the case study

For the tote transfer, a simple hand that can hook under the lip, with pads for a light pinch to steady the tote, covers the task. A fully dexterous hand would work too, but it adds actuators, sensors and cost the task does not need. The choice is made in the case study (4.5, Planned).

## 2.7.5 Validation and Failure Modes

### Tests

| Test | What it shows | Pass criterion (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Grasp success rate | Fraction of attempts that lift and hold, over a stated object set and spread of poses | Above the task's target, with the object set and poses written down |
| Pull-out force | Force needed to pull the object from the grasp | Above the design load times the safety factor |
| Slip detection time | Time from slip starting to the hand reacting | Fast enough to re-grip before the object is lost |
| Cycle life | Wear of pads, fingers, tendons and cups over repeated grasps | Grip and sensing within limits after the target number of cycles |
| Ingress and cleaning | Whether the hand survives the site's dust, liquids and cleaning | Passes the IP code the site needs ([[2.6 - Mechanical Architecture and Structures\|2.6]] §2.6.2) |

### Failure modes

| Failure | Cause | How it is detected | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Slip | Low friction, too little squeeze, sudden acceleration | Touch or force sensing, object moving relative to the hand | Squeeze harder or slow down; set down and re-grasp |
| Dropped object | Slip not caught, grasp failure | Loss of load at the wrist force sensor | Stop, report, and ask for help if needed ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map\|1.4]] §1.4.5) |
| Pad wear | Abrasion over many grasps | Falling grip success, inspection | Replace pads as a service item |
| Jammed finger | Debris, broken tendon or gear | Finger not reaching its commanded position | Stop using the hand; service |
| Damaged suction cup | Wear, cuts, sharp edges | Vacuum not reaching its level, or leaking | Replace the cup |
| Touch sensor failure | Worn or torn skin | Missing or implausible signals | Fall back to force-only grasping; service |

## 2.7.6 Industry Progress

| Date | Platform | Claim | Evidence stage | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-07-09 | 1X NEO | 25 DoF hand (22 in fingers and palm, 3 at the wrist), tendon-driven at about 5:1 to 15:1, fully backdrivable; IP68; peak 3.5 N m at the thumb base, 2.6 N m at the finger knuckles, 45 N fingertip force; tactile sensing of pressure and shear; "hundreds" built, capacity for 10,000 a year | Vendor-reported | [1X](https://1x.tech/discover/neos-hands) |
| Undated | Boston Dynamics Atlas | Four fingers, 13 DoF, directly actuated with one actuator type; tactile sensors on fingertips and palm; no fifth finger, to save "three more actuators" | Vendor-reported | [Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/blog/robot-hands-for-modern-ai-and-real-work/) |
| Accessed 2026-11-22 | Agility Digit 5 | "swappable gripper design" | Vendor-reported; product in development | [Agility](https://www.agilityrobotics.com/solutions/digit-5) |
| 2013 to 2017 | Yale OpenHand | Open-source, 3D-printed, tendon-driven underactuated hands | Research, open source | [Yale OpenHand](https://www.eng.yale.edu/grablab/openhand/index.html) |

**What this shows.** The two most detailed vendor hands take opposite routes to the same goal. 1X puts the motors in the forearm and drives many joints through tendons; Boston Dynamics drives fewer joints directly. Both stress backdrivability, so the fingers can feel contact through their motors, and both add touch sensing. 1X claims component life of millions of cycles but publishes no failure rates. Agility, whose Digit is aimed at warehouse work, describes a swappable gripper rather than a dexterous hand.

## 2.7.7 Bottlenecks

- **Dexterous hands: robustness, cost and life.** A hand with 13 to 25 actuators has many more parts that can fail than a gripper with one or two. No maker found publishes field failure rates.
- **Touch sensing that lasts.** The soft materials touch sensors need "often lack durability under prolonged use or under harsh conditions" ([Luo et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.11261)), and high fabrication cost "has hindered their widespread adoption".
- **Generality against reliability.** A task-specific gripper is reliable on its task and useless off it. A general hand can do many tasks, each less reliably. Boston Dynamics argues that no hand is truly general; the bet is that tools make up the difference.

## 2.7.8 Implications for the Tote Transfer

- **Simple is enough.** A hook under the lip, steadied by a light pinch, holds the tote without depending on friction (§2.7.4). A parallel gripper or a simple underactuated hand does the job.
- **What a dexterous hand would add.** Handling totes without lips, opening flaps, or picking items out of the tote. If the task stays tote transfer, those are costs without benefit: more actuators, more sensors, more to wear out.
- **Grip margin.** A friction-only grasp needs about 118 N of squeeze per hand in good conditions and nearly 200 N on a dusty tote. A hand chosen for dexterity must still meet that.
- **Simpler alternative.** A fixed robot cell with a tote gripper built for one tote type, fed by conveyors.

### Contrast: fixture loading

The fixture-loading case is where the hand gets hard. The part is thin, sharp-edged sheet metal that flexes when held, and it must be seated on locating pins within ±1 mm ([[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]] §1.5.8). A friction pinch bends the panel; edge grasps need precise finger placement; magnets and suction cups work but must be laid out for each part. A fixed robot cell solves this with tooling built for each part, often swapped by a tool changer. A humanoid needs either the same kind of tool, carried and swapped, which gives up some of the humanoid's generality, or a dexterous hand with touch sensing good enough to feel the part seat on the pins (3.8, Planned).
