**Scope.** This chapter covers how a humanoid stores energy and delivers it safely. Topics:

- cells and how they combine into a pack;
- the battery management system;
- estimating how much charge is left;
- battery chemistry;
- the main energy strategies: one large pack, opportunity charging, swapping, and solar;
- testing, failure modes, and safety and transport rules.

Power distribution beyond the pack terminals is in [[2.2 - Power Distribution and Protection|2.2]], and the whole-robot energy budget in 2.8 (Planned). Numbers in worked examples are **illustrative estimates** unless a source sits next to them.

## 2.1.1 Purpose and Interfaces

The battery is the robot's only energy store. It has to supply the average power of a working shift, the much higher peaks of lifting and stepping, and enough reserve to reach a safe posture when something goes wrong.

- **Energy:** a pack voltage range from full to empty, sagging further under load. It must supply continuous current for the average draw and peak current for lifts and steps, and it holds a usable energy, which is less than the rated energy (§2.1.2).
- **Data:** the pack reports state of charge, state of health, cell voltages and temperatures, and fault flags to the robot's computer.
- **Commands and feedback:** the robot asks the pack to close or open its main contactors (heavy-duty switches), enable charging, and give warning before it must shut down.
- **Timing:** a short circuit or a cell overheating has to be detected and the pack isolated within milliseconds.
- **Physical:** the pack is one of the heaviest single parts. It is usually carried in the torso, where it moves the center of mass ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.4). It must survive a fall and, if swappable, be reachable by a person or by the robot itself.

## 2.1.2 Fundamentals

### Cells, packs, and the numbers that describe them

A lithium-ion **cell** is the basic unit. Each cell has:

- **Voltage:** set by its chemistry, a few volts per cell.
- **Capacity:** the charge it holds, in ampere-hours (Ah).
- **Energy:** voltage times capacity, in watt-hours (Wh).

Two ratios compare cells and packs: **specific energy** (Wh/kg) for mass, and **energy density** (Wh/L) for volume. Both are lower for a whole pack than for its cells, because the pack adds structure, wiring, cooling, and electronics.

Cells connected in **series** add their voltages; cells in **parallel** add their capacities. A pack described as "14s4p" has 14 cells in series, with each series position made of 4 cells in parallel.

**C-rate** expresses current relative to capacity. A 1C current would empty the pack in one hour, 2C in half an hour, and 0.5C in two hours. High C-rates in either direction, discharging hard or charging fast, heat the cells and age them faster.

### Behaviour under load

Every cell has **internal resistance**. Under load, its voltage sags below its resting value and the lost voltage becomes heat. The heat is proportional to the square of the current, so a pack that delivers twice the current makes four times the internal heat. Internal resistance rises in the cold and as the cell ages, which is why a cold or old pack may shut down under a peak load it would once have handled. The drives and the rest of the robot must keep working across the whole sagging voltage range (2.2, Planned).

### Rated versus usable capacity

Packs are rarely run from completely full to completely empty. Running cells to their extremes shortens their life, and a legged robot must keep a reserve to reach a safe posture rather than fall ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.5). The **usable** energy is therefore a fraction of the rated energy. How large a fraction depends on the chemistry: the IEA notes that LFP cells can reach "100% state of charge when required without significant degradation", while "NMC batteries are typically limited to 80% to preserve long-term performance" ([IEA, Global EV Outlook 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries); see [[Source Note - IEA Global EV Outlook Battery Chapters]]). Those figures describe electric vehicles, but the principle carries over to robots.

### Ageing

Cells lose capacity and gain internal resistance over time in two ways:

- **Cycle ageing:** comes from use, and is faster with deep cycles, high C-rates, and heat.
- **Calendar ageing:** happens even in storage, and is faster at high temperature and high state of charge.

A pack sized for a shift on day one has to still cover it after years of ageing, so packs are sized with an end-of-life margin.

### State of charge and state of health

**State of charge** (SoC) is how full the pack is, as a percentage. **State of health** (SoH) is how much of its original capacity remains. Neither can be measured directly; both are estimated by three main methods:

- **Coulomb counting** integrates the current in and out. It is accurate over short periods but drifts as small measurement errors add up.
- **Voltage-based estimation** reads charge from the resting voltage. It works poorly under load, and some chemistries, LFP in particular, have a very flat voltage curve over most of their range.
- **Model-based estimation** combines both with a model of the cell, correcting the count whenever the voltage gives good information.

An SoC error matters more for a humanoid than for most machines. If the estimate says 15% remains and the truth is 3%, a wheeled robot stops where it is, while a biped may not have the energy to crouch safely.

### What the battery management system does

The **battery management system** (BMS) is the electronics and firmware that keep the pack safe and report on it. Its main jobs:

- **Monitoring:** measures every cell group's voltage, the pack current, and temperatures at several points.
- **Protection:** opens the contactors on over-voltage, under-voltage, overcurrent, or overtemperature.
- **Balancing:** keeps the cells in a series string at the same charge, because the weakest cell limits the whole string.
- **Estimation:** computes state of charge and state of health.
- **Charge control:** tells the charger what current and voltage to deliver.
- **Communication:** reports to the robot's computer and to the charger, including warnings with enough lead time for the robot to stop work and reach a safe posture.

## 2.1.3 Design Alternatives and Tradeoffs

### Chemistry

Two families of lithium-ion chemistry dominate:

|                 | Nickel-based (NMC, NCA)                    | Lithium iron phosphate (LFP)                                        |
| --------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Specific energy | Higher                                     | "about one-fifth lower by mass (Wh/kg)" than NMC packs (IEA 2025)   |
| Energy density  | Higher                                     | "about one-third lower by volume (Wh/L)" (IEA 2025)                 |
| Usable range    | "typically limited to 80%" (IEA 2025)      | Can reach 100% when required (IEA 2025)                             |
| Cost            | Higher                                     | Packs "more than 40% cheaper on average" per kWh in 2025 (IEA 2026) |
| Cold weather    | Better                                     | "typically less effective" in cold climates (IEA 2025)              |
| Voltage curve   | Slopes with charge, easier to estimate SoC | Very flat, harder to estimate SoC                                   |

Sources: [IEA, Global EV Outlook 2025](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2025/electric-vehicle-batteries) and [2026](https://www.iea.org/reports/global-ev-outlook-2026/electric-vehicle-batteries) (see [[Source Note - IEA Global EV Outlook Battery Chapters]]). The figures are for electric-vehicle packs, which are far larger than a robot's. The IEA adds that part of LFP's cost advantage "reflects the lower energy density requirements of stationary storage applications".

LFP is also widely regarded as more tolerant of heat and abuse than nickel-rich chemistries. This library has not yet checked a primary source that compares the two directly, so the difference is not quantified here.

For a humanoid the trade is sharp. Mass is expensive, because every kilogram in the torso is carried on every step, which favours the higher specific energy of nickel-based cells. But the robot works beside people, so thermal safety and cost matter too, and they favour LFP. Robots that charge or swap often need less stored energy, which makes LFP's lower specific energy easier to accept.

### Pack voltage

For a given power, a higher pack voltage means lower current, so lighter wiring and lower resistive losses. Above certain voltages, stricter electrical safety rules apply. [[2.2 - Power Distribution and Protection|2.2]] §2.2.3 develops this trade.

### Energy strategy

| Strategy             | How it works                                                                             | Gains                                                                                                                                            | Costs                                                                                           |
| -------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| One large pack       | Carry enough energy for the whole shift                                                  | Simple; no charging during work                                                                                                                  | Heavy; the mass is carried on every step                                                        |
| Opportunity charging | Smaller pack, topped up at a dock during breaks or idle time                             | Lighter robot                                                                                                                                    | Charging time comes out of scheduled hours; needs fast-charge-tolerant cells and docks          |
| Battery swapping     | The robot, or a person, exchanges an empty pack for a charged one                        | Short downtime; packs charge slowly off the robot, which is gentler on them                                                                      | Spare packs, a swap station, and a mechanism to do it; a biped needs power during the swap      |
| Solar on the robot   | Panels on the body top up the pack while it works, alongside one of the strategies above | Extends run time between charges outdoors, with no downtime, in the event of failure to reach a charging station solar can be used as a recovery | Small gain outdoors and almost none indoors; panel mass could instead be battery (worked below) |


The first three are the main choices, and a design can combine them. A robot can opportunity-charge during short idle periods and still swap packs when a long task is queued. UBTECH says its Walker S2 "can autonomously choose battery swap or charge mode according to the task priorities" (§2.1.6).

### Charging interface

A robot that docks by itself needs charging contacts it can reach without help: exposed contacts or a guided connector at the dock, or inductive charging with no metal contact. Exposed contacts are simple, but they must tolerate misalignment, wear, and dirt, and be safe when a person touches them. A plugged connector is robust and carries high current well, but needs a person or a precise mechanism to plug it in.

### The alternative

Wheeled mobile robots in warehouses have used opportunity charging at docks for years, and their problem is easier. A statically stable machine can stop anywhere without falling, rolling uses little energy, and the battery can sit low in the base, where its mass helps stability. A humanoid has to solve the same scheduling problem with a heavier penalty for carrying energy and a harder requirement for reserve.

## 2.1.4 Sizing or Worked Example

### Shift energy and pack size for the tote case (illustrative estimate)

**Assumptions** for one 60 s tote cycle ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.6):

- **Baseline:** the robot draws $P_{\text{base}} = 300\ \text{W}$ whenever it is on, for standing, compute, sensors, and cooling.
- **Walking:** 20 m per cycle costs 1.9 Wh for an efficient walker ([[1.2 - What Is a Humanoid Robot|1.2]] §1.2.3).
- **Lift:** about 0.05 Wh, as follows. Raising the 10 kg tote 0.6 m and about 8 kg of arm and torso mass 0.3 m is $9.81 \times (10 \times 0.6 + 8 \times 0.3) = 82.4\ \text{J}$ of work. At an assumed battery-to-joint efficiency of 0.475, that is $82.4 / 0.475 = 173\ \text{J} = 0.048\ \text{Wh}$.
- **Rate and shift:** 60 cycles per hour, over the 8 h shift of requirement T-11 ([[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]] §1.5.8).
- **Usable fraction:** 0.8 of rated energy.
- **Pack specific energy:** 150 Wh/kg for the whole pack, an assumed round value.

**Energy per cycle, per hour, and per shift:**

$$
\begin{aligned}
E_{\text{cycle}} &= 300\ \text{W} \times 60\ \text{s} + 1.9\ \text{Wh} + 0.05\ \text{Wh} = 5.0 + 1.9 + 0.05 = 6.95\ \text{Wh} \\
\bar{P} &= 60\ \text{cycles/h} \times 6.95\ \text{Wh} = 417\ \text{W} \\
E_{\text{shift}} &= 417\ \text{W} \times 8\ \text{h} = 3{,}336\ \text{Wh}
\end{aligned}
$$

where $\bar{P}$ is the average power over a working hour.

**A pack for the whole shift:**

$$
E_{\text{rated}} = \frac{E_{\text{shift}}}{0.8} = \frac{3{,}336\ \text{Wh}}{0.8} = 4{,}170\ \text{Wh}
\qquad
m_{\text{pack}} = \frac{4{,}170\ \text{Wh}}{150\ \text{Wh/kg}} = 27.8\ \text{kg}
$$

That is close to half the mass of a 60 kg robot, carried on every step, and adding mass raises the walking and standing energy, which raises the pack size again. A full-shift pack is not a practical design for a human-sized robot at this power.

**A 2 h pack with opportunity charging:**

$$
E_{\text{rated}} = \frac{417\ \text{W} \times 2\ \text{h}}{0.8} = 1{,}043\ \text{Wh}
\qquad
m_{\text{pack}} = \frac{1{,}043\ \text{Wh}}{150\ \text{Wh/kg}} = 7.0\ \text{kg}
$$

Recharging the 834 Wh used takes $t_{\text{charge}} = 834\ \text{Wh} / P_{\text{charger}}$, ignoring charging losses and the slower final part of a charge. The fraction of time spent charging is $t_{\text{charge}} / (t_{\text{run}} + t_{\text{charge}})$:

| Strategy | Downtime per 2 h of work | Fraction of time not working |
|---|---|---|
| Charge at 2 kW (about 2C) | 25 min | 17% |
| Charge at 4 kW (about 4C) | 12.5 min | 9.4% |
| Swap in 3 min | 3 min | 2.4% |

**Tote hook: charging against availability.** Requirement T-7 asks for at least 85% availability over scheduled hours ([[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]] §1.5.8). That 15% allowance must also cover faults, recoveries, and maintenance. Charging at 2 kW uses all of it and more. Charging at 4 kW uses most of it, and needs cells that tolerate 4C charging over thousands of cycles. Swapping leaves most of the allowance free, at the cost of spare packs and a swap station. This is why vendors are investing in both fast charging and swapping (§2.1.6).

**Sensitivity.** The baseline dominates:

- **Baseline 20% lower** (240 W): cycle energy falls to 5.95 Wh, 14% less.
- **Walking 20% lower:** cycle energy falls by 5.5%.
- **Lift:** makes no visible difference.

Battery size is set by what the robot draws just being on and upright, as [[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.2 argues. The most effective way to shrink the pack is to cut baseline power, through more efficient standing, compute, and cooling (2.8 and 2.10, Planned).

### Solar as a supplementary charge (illustrative estimate)

The question is how much run time body-mounted panels add to the 2 h pack above, and whether the same mass spent on battery would add more. Solar modules are rated at standard test conditions: "solar irradiance of 1000 W/m2, spectral conditions Am1.5, and cell temperature of 25 °C" ([Penn State AE 868](https://courses.ems.psu.edu/ae868/book/export/html/879); see [[Source Note - Penn State AE868 PV Datasheet Standard Test Conditions]]).

**Assumptions:**

- **Panel:** $A_{\text{pv}} = 0.3\ \text{m}^2$ on the torso, shoulders, and head, with module efficiency $\eta_{\text{pv}} = 0.20$.
- **Panel mass:** about 3 kg per square metre including mounting, so about 0.9 kg.
- **Load:** average draw $\bar{P} = 417\ \text{W}$, with $E_{\text{use}} = 834\ \text{Wh}$ usable in the 2 h pack.
- **Best-case light:** full rated sun on the panels the whole time.
- **Realistic outdoor light:** the panels average a quarter of their rated output over the shift. Walking turns them away from the sun, the sun is low for part of the day, and clouds pass.
- **Indoor light:** about $5\ \text{W/m}^2$ reaches the panels.

**Solar power at rated sun:**

$$
P_{\text{pv}} = G\, A_{\text{pv}}\, \eta_{\text{pv}} = 1000\ \text{W/m}^2 \times 0.3\ \text{m}^2 \times 0.20 = 60\ \text{W}
$$

where $G$ is the irradiance. Solar lowers the net draw from the pack, so the run time on one charge becomes

$$
t_{\text{run}} = \frac{E_{\text{use}}}{\bar{P} - P_{\text{pv}}}
$$

| Case | Solar power | Run time on the 2 h pack | Gain per charge |
|---|---|---|---|
| No solar | 0 W | 2.00 h | none |
| Best case (rated sun all the time) | 60 W | 2.34 h | +20 min (+17%) |
| Realistic outdoors (a quarter of rated) | 15 W | 2.07 h | +4.5 min (+4%) |
| Indoors | 0.3 W | 2.00 h | under 0.1% |

**Compared with the same mass in battery.** The 0.9 kg of panels could instead be 0.9 kg more battery. At 150 Wh/kg that is 135 Wh, which adds $135 / 417\ \text{h} \approx 19$ min per charge anywhere, indoors or out, day or night. Over a full 8 h outdoor shift, the realistic solar case collects $15\ \text{W} \times 8\ \text{h} = 120\ \text{Wh}$, still less than the extra battery stores. The best case would collect 480 Wh, but no walking robot keeps its panels square to the sun all day. Solar beats the extra battery only for long hours in strong, direct sun.

## 2.1.5 Validation and Failure Modes

### Tests

| Test | What it shows | Pass criterion (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Capacity at the shift's discharge rate and temperature | Usable energy in real use, not at a gentle lab rate | Usable energy covers the planned run time with reserve |
| Internal resistance, new and aged | Voltage sag and heating at peak current | Voltage stays above the drives' minimum at peak load |
| Thermal test on a shift-like load profile | Cell temperatures during real work and charging | Hottest cell stays below the cell maker's limit |
| Cycle life on the real charge and discharge pattern | How fast capacity fades with fast charging | Meets end-of-life capacity after the planned number of cycles |
| SoC accuracy over a shift | How far the estimate drifts | Error small enough that the reserve is never breached |
| Swap or dock cycle test | Mechanical and contact wear | Thousands of swaps or docks without contact faults |

### Transport and safety standards

Lithium batteries must pass the UN transport tests before they can be shipped. The UN Manual of Tests and Criteria, section 38.3, covers lithium metal and lithium-ion cells and batteries under UN numbers 3090, 3091, 3480, and 3481. "All cell types shall be subjected to tests T.1 to T.6 and T.8", and rechargeable batteries to T.1 to T.5 and T.7 ([NRC Canada record of UN 38.3](https://csres-cnrse.nrc-cnrc.gc.ca/en/node/428); see [[Source Note - UN Manual of Tests and Criteria 38.3]]). A swappable humanoid pack is shipped on its own, so this applies to it directly.

For product safety, IEC 62133-2:2017 "specifies requirements and tests for the safe operation of portable sealed secondary lithium cells and batteries" ([IEC webstore](https://webstore.iec.ch/en/publication/32662); see [[Source Note - IEC 62133-2 2017]]). Its scope is portable applications. Whether it, or a standard written for larger batteries, is the right basis for a humanoid's pack is a question for 5.3 (Planned).

### Failure modes

| Failure | Cause | How it is detected | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Thermal runaway | Internal short, damage in a fall, overcharge, overheating | Cell temperature rise, voltage collapse, gas or smoke sensing | Isolate the pack, warn people, move away from people if possible; prevention is the main defence |
| Cell imbalance | Cells age unevenly | Spread of cell voltages | Balance, then limit the usable range, then replace the pack |
| Low-voltage cutoff under peak load | Cold or aged pack, high current | Voltage sag below threshold | Limit peak power, warn the task layer before it happens |
| SoC estimate error | Drift in coulomb counting, flat voltage curve | Disagreement between methods, surprise cutoffs | Recalibrate at full charge; keep a larger reserve |
| Contact or connector wear | Docking and swapping cycles | Contact resistance, heating at contacts | Inspect and replace contacts as a service item |

**The legged robot's extra requirement.** Every failure response has to leave the robot in a safe posture. The BMS must warn early enough, and keep enough energy in reserve, for the robot to stop work, crouch or kneel, and only then shut down ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.5). A protective cutoff that drops power instantly on a standing biped causes a fall. So the pack's own protection and the robot's safe-stop behaviour have to be designed together (5.3, Planned).

**What this shows.** The vendors have converged on short run times and very short downtime instead of all-shift packs:

- **Atlas:** about 4 h per battery, with a swap in under 3 min.
- **Walker S2:** a swap in under 3 min.
- **Digit 5:** 90 min of runtime with a 9 min charge.

That agrees with the worked example in §2.1.4. A 9 min charge after 90 min of work spends $9 / 99 \approx 9\%$ of the time charging, about the same as the 4 kW case there. A 3 min swap every 4 h is about 1%. None of these figures yet comes with independent measurement, fleet data, or the conditions behind "typical use".

## 2.1.7 Bottlenecks

- **Runtime against mass.** At pack-level specific energies, a full shift of work costs a large share of a human-sized robot's mass (§2.1.4). Today's designs work around this rather than solving it: short runs plus fast charging or swapping.
- **Fast charging against cell life.** Charging at several C heats and ages cells faster. Vendors claim fast-charge chemistries with long life, but no cycle-life figures are disclosed in the sources above.
- **Swapping mechanisms.** A robot that swaps its own pack needs a second power source to stay upright during the swap. Walker S2's dual-battery design is one answer. The swap mechanism also adds parts that can wear and fail.
- **Safety approval.** Large lithium packs in machines that work beside people, and that can fall, raise questions about pack crash protection and which standards apply. These remain unsettled for humanoids (5.3, Planned).

## 2.1.8 Implications for the Tote Transfer

- **Availability.** Charging is the largest predictable drain on the availability budget. A 2 h pack charged at 2 kW cannot meet T-7 on its own; fast charging or swapping can (§2.1.4).
- **Throughput.** Successful transfers per scheduled hour fall in proportion to time spent charging, so a 9% charging share costs about 9% of throughput ([[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]] §1.5.5).
- **Energy per successful transfer.** About 7 Wh per cycle in this example, most of it baseline, rising with every failed attempt and retry. A wheeled mobile manipulator on the same flat floor would spend far less on standing and moving, and its pack mass would help rather than hurt stability. This is one of the costs a humanoid must earn back through access and flexibility ([[1.3 - Why Build a Humanoid|1.3]] §1.3.4).
- **Scheduling.** Swaps and charges should fall in natural gaps such as shift breaks and low-demand periods. That ties battery strategy to task scheduling in the warehouse (4.4, Planned).

## Open Questions

1. **Can a human-sized robot carry enough energy for a full shift of physical work at an acceptable mass?** At current pack specific energies the answer is no for a robot drawing a few hundred watts (§2.1.4), and vendors design around it with fast charging and swapping (§2.1.6). Whether better cells or much lower baseline power will change that is not yet known.
