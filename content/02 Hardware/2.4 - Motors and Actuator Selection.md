**Scope.** This chapter covers how a humanoid's joints are powered. Topics:

- how a brushless motor turns current into torque;
- what limits it: heat, speed and peak current;
- the main actuator architectures, and how to choose one per joint.

Drives are in [[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]], gearboxes and joint modules in [[2.5 - Transmissions and Joint Modules|2.5]], and cooling in 2.8 (Planned). Numbers in worked examples are **illustrative estimates** unless a source sits next to them.

## 2.4.1 Purpose and Interfaces

An actuator is the motor plus whatever sits between it and the joint: a gearbox, a spring, or a screw and linkage. It must deliver the torque and speed each motion needs, often for long stretches, without overheating, and it should survive impacts and falls.

- **Energy:** phase current in from the drive, torque and speed out at the joint, heat from losses in the windings and gearing.
- **Data:** rotor position from the motor encoder, joint position from an output encoder where fitted, and winding or housing temperature.
- **Commands and feedback:** a torque or current command through the drive ([[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]] §2.3.1).
- **Timing:** mechanical response in milliseconds; thermal response from tens of seconds (windings) to many minutes (housing).
- **Physical:** mass and size inside the limb, rotor inertia, output bearing loads, and mounting into the structure ([[2.6 - Mechanical Architecture and Structures|2.6]]).

## 2.4.2 Fundamentals

### Brushless motors

Humanoid joints use **permanent-magnet brushless motors**. Magnets sit on the rotor, and the drive switches current through windings on the stator, so there are no brushes to wear. TI notes that humanoids "can incorporate motors like PMSM motors when requiring high power levels", while brushed motors suit "some low power cases such as hand and finger control" ([TI SLLA659](https://www.ti.com/document-viewer/lit/html/SLLA659)). A PMSM is a permanent-magnet synchronous motor, the brushless type driven with field-oriented control ([[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]] §2.3.2).

### Torque constant and back-EMF

Two constants describe a motor electrically:

$$
\tau = k_t\, I, \qquad V_{\text{emf}} = k_e\, \omega
$$

Here $\tau$ is motor torque in N m, $k_t$ the **torque constant** in N m/A, $I$ the torque-producing current in amps, $V_{\text{emf}}$ the **back-EMF** (the voltage a spinning motor generates) in volts, $k_e$ the back-EMF constant in V s/rad, and $\omega$ the motor speed in rad/s. In consistent SI units the two constants are the same physical quantity.

Back-EMF sets the top speed: as the motor spins faster, its back-EMF rises toward the bus voltage, leaving less voltage to push current in. For hobby motors this is often given as a **KV rating** in rpm per volt. The approximate no-load speed is KV times the supply voltage.

**A warning about conventions.** Lee et al. show that torque constants are quoted per phase, per line, or on the rotating axes used by field-oriented control, and that these differ by factors such as $\sqrt{3/2}$. They warn that "the winding resistance provided in manufacturer datasheets is often the terminal resistance", and that a mismatch can make heat estimates wrong "by as much as 100%" ([Lee et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.00080)). Before sizing, put the torque constant, current and resistance into one convention. This library uses phase-current amplitude throughout, with copper loss $1.5\, I^2 R_{\text{ph}}$ ([[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]] §2.3.4).

### Torque against heat: the motor constant

The winding resistance turns current into heat. With torque proportional to current, copper loss grows with the square of torque:

$$
P_{\text{cu}} = 1.5\, I^2 R_{\text{ph}} = \left( \frac{\tau}{K_m} \right)^2, \qquad K_m = \frac{k_t}{\sqrt{1.5\, R_{\text{ph}}}}
$$

Here $P_{\text{cu}}$ is copper loss in watts, $R_{\text{ph}}$ the phase resistance in ohms, and $K_m$ the **motor constant** in N m per square root of watt. Allient describes the motor constant's units as "Nm per square root of watts dissipated in the form of heat", and notes that "it only considers the copper losses" ([Allient](https://allient.com/blogs/effective-motor-constant/)). A higher $K_m$ means more torque for the same heat. Doubling the torque quadruples the heat. That is why holding a load still is expensive: a held joint does no mechanical work, but its windings heat at full rate ([[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]] §2.3.4).

### Continuous and peak torque

- **Continuous torque** is what the motor can produce indefinitely without the windings passing their temperature limit, given a stated cooling condition.
- **Peak torque** is what it can produce briefly. It is limited by drive current, magnet saturation, and the thermal time constant: windings heat in seconds, the housing in minutes.

So a joint can lift hard for a second or two, but not hold that torque for long. The duty cycle, meaning the torque over time, decides whether a motor is big enough, not the peak alone. 2.8 (Planned) models the heating.

### Mass and inertia

Motor mass sits in the limb, where every kilogram costs energy to swing and loads every joint closer to the body. Rotor inertia, multiplied by the square of the gear ratio, is what the joint feels on impact ([[2.5 - Transmissions and Joint Modules|2.5]] §2.5.4). Both push toward light motors placed close to the body.

## 2.4.3 Design Alternatives and Tradeoffs

### Actuator architectures

| Architecture                  | How it works                                                                             | Strengths                                                                              | Weaknesses                                                                  |
| ----------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Quasi-direct drive (QDD)      | Large-diameter motor with a low gear ratio (10:1 in the Zhu et al. actuator below)       | Backdrivable, tolerates impacts, torque sensed from motor current                      | Large, heavy motor for the torque; high current while holding               |
| High-ratio drive              | Smaller motor with a high-ratio gearbox ([[2.5 - Transmissions and Joint Modules\|2.5]]) | Compact and strong for its mass; little holding current                                | Stiff, hard to push back, high reflected inertia on impact                  |
| Series elastic actuator (SEA) | A spring between gearbox and load; spring deflection measures force                      | Clean force control, protects the gearbox from shocks                                  | Lower bandwidth, more parts, the spring adds compliance to position control |
| Linear screw actuator         | Motor turns a ball or roller screw that pushes a joint through a linkage                 | High force in a slim package; the linkage can vary the effective ratio over the motion | More parts, nonlinear behaviour, screws can be hard to backdrive            |

**Quasi-direct drive.** The MIT Cheetah work introduced "a proprioceptive actuation paradigm", which "uses collocated force control at the joints": the motor current itself is the force measurement ([Seok, Wensing et al., 2017](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/119863)). It claims "a unique combination of high torque density, high-bandwidth force control, and the ability to mitigate impacts through backdrivability". **Backdrivability** means external force can turn the motor back through the gearing. The authors define an "impact mitigation factor" to quantify it.

**Series elastic.** Williamson describes the trade directly: the series elastic element "trades off achievable bandwidth for gains in stable, low noise force control, and protection against shock loads" ([Williamson, 1995](https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/6776)).

**Linear screw.** Ma et al. drive a humanoid knee with a linear actuator through a linkage, so the reduction ratio changes over the knee's motion: "a dynamically decreasing reduction ratio" ([Ma et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12314)).

**Hydraulic actuation** is not covered. No checked source on hydraulic humanoid actuators was found.

### Choosing per joint

Different joints have different loads, speeds and duty cycles, so one robot often mixes architectures.

- **Hips and knees:** the highest torques, especially when crouching under a load. They also take impacts from every step. This favours quasi-direct drive or linear actuators with a variable ratio.
- **Shoulders and elbows:** long holds with the arm extended. Holding favours a higher ratio or a higher motor constant to keep holding heat down.
- **Wrists and hands:** low torque, tight space. Small motors with high ratios, or brushed motors for fingers, as TI notes.
- **Ankles:** high torque near the floor where mass is most costly to swing, so the motor is often placed higher up and connected by a linkage.

## 2.4.4 Sizing or Worked Example

All values are **illustrative estimates** unless marked otherwise. This example replaces the shoulder-torque derivation once planned for 1.5.

### The candidate actuator

The check uses a real, published actuator as the candidate: the cycloidal quasi-direct-drive actuator of Zhu et al., with a "10:1 cycloidal gearbox", continuous torque 37.5 N m, measured peak torque 89.9 N m, and mass 1.40 kg ([Zhu et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16591), author-measured). The authors do not state the cooling conditions behind the continuous rating, so the thermal check uses this library's illustrative motor values from [[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]] §2.3.4 instead: $k_t = 0.2\ \text{N\,m/A}$ and $R_{\text{ph}} = 0.1\ \Omega$ at the motor, so $K_m = 0.2 / \sqrt{0.15} = 0.516\ \text{N\,m}/\sqrt{\text{W}}$.

### Shoulder

**Static torque.** The 10 kg tote is shared by two arms, so 5 kg per arm, held 0.5 m in front of the shoulder at the shelf. The arm itself, about 4 kg, acts at about 0.25 m:

$$
\tau_{\text{static}} = 9.81 \times (5 \times 0.5 + 4 \times 0.25) = 34.3\ \text{N\,m}
$$

**Peak torque.** Accelerating the tote upward and starting the arm swing add to the static torque. A **dynamic factor** of 1.5 (illustrative; the real value depends on the lift trajectory, 3.6, Planned) gives $1.5 \times 34.3 = 51.5\ \text{N\,m}$.

**Carry torque.** Carried close to the body, at 0.2 m for the tote and 0.15 m for the arm, the torque drops to $9.81 \times (5 \times 0.2 + 4 \times 0.15) = 15.7\ \text{N\,m}$.

### Knee

The robot crouches to pick the tote from 0.4 m. Take a 70 kg robot plus the 10 kg tote, with the combined centre of mass 0.15 m behind the knees in the crouch, shared by two legs:

$$
\tau_{\text{knee}} = \frac{80 \times 9.81 \times 0.15}{2} = 58.9\ \text{N\,m}
$$

Rising out of the crouch with a dynamic factor of 1.3 gives $76.5\ \text{N\,m}$.

### Torque check

| Joint and phase | Torque needed | Against continuous 37.5 N m | Against peak 89.9 N m |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shoulder, carrying close | 15.7 N m | 42% | 17% |
| Shoulder, holding at the shelf | 34.3 N m | 91% | 38% |
| Shoulder, lift peak | 51.5 N m | 137% (brief) | 57% |
| Knee, crouched | 58.9 N m | 157% | 66% |
| Knee, rising | 76.5 N m | 204% (brief) | 85% |

### Speed check

Joint speeds in the lift are low. The hand rising at about 0.4 m/s at 0.5 m from the shoulder is $0.4 / 0.5 = 0.8\ \text{rad/s}$ at the joint, or 8 rad/s at the motor through 10:1. For comparison, the drone motor in Berkeley Humanoid Lite's larger actuator is a "M6C12 150KV" ([Chi et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17249)). On a 48 V bus, $150 \times 48 = 7{,}200\ \text{rpm} \approx 754\ \text{rad/s}$ no-load. Speed is not the limit for lifting. It matters for fast leg swings, which 3.7 (Planned) covers.

### Heat over one cycle

Copper loss scales with torque squared. At the motor, the shoulder's 34.3 N m is about 3.4 N m through 10:1, so $(3.4 / 0.516)^2 \approx 43\ \text{W}$, matching [[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics|2.3]] §2.3.4. The same scaling gives each phase of the 60 s cycle:

| Phase (shoulder) | Torque | Duration | Copper loss | Heat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lift | 51.5 N m | 1.5 s | 98 W | 147 J |
| Hold at shelf | 34.3 N m | 3 s | 43 W | 130 J |
| Carry close | 15.7 N m | 20 s | 9 W | 182 J |
| Arm lowered, walking empty | about 0 | 35.5 s | about 0 | about 0 |
| **Cycle** | | 60 s | **7.6 W average** | **459 J** |

| Phase (knee) | Torque | Duration | Copper loss | Heat |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Crouched | 58.9 N m | 3 s | 128 W | 384 J |
| Rising | 76.5 N m | 1.5 s | 216 W | 324 J |
| **Cycle** | | 60 s | **11.8 W average** | **708 J** |

The knee table counts only the crouch. Walking adds stance torque on every step, which 3.7 and 2.8 (Planned) add.

**What it shows.**

- **Averaged over the cycle, heat is modest.** A 7.6 W average shoulder loss is far below the 43 W of holding at the shelf continuously. The duty cycle, not the peak, sets the thermal size.
- **The shoulder's limit is holding at reach.** Holding at the shelf uses 91% of the candidate's continuous rating. A task that held the tote at full reach for minutes, not seconds, would overheat it. Holding close to the body cuts the loss by a factor of about five.
- **The knee saturates first.** Crouching under the tote needs 157% of the continuous rating and 85% of the peak. This actuator is too small for the knee in a deep crouch. The options are a larger actuator, a higher ratio, a linear actuator with a variable ratio, or a pick posture that keeps the knees closer under the load.

## 2.4.5 Validation and Failure Modes

### Tests

| Test | What it shows | Pass criterion (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Dynamometer map | Torque, speed and efficiency across the operating range, driven by the real drive | Covers every operating point of the duty cycle with margin |
| Torque constant check | Torque per amp in the convention the controller uses | Matches the model within a few percent |
| Thermal soak on the duty cycle | Winding and housing temperature over a shift | Stays below the winding limit at maximum ambient |
| Backdrive torque | Torque needed to turn the joint from outside | Low enough for the compliance the joint needs |
| Impact test | Peak force and any damage when the output is struck | No damage; force below the gearbox rating ([[2.5 - Transmissions and Joint Modules\|2.5]] §2.5.5) |

Zhu et al. report the kind of numbers such tests produce: backlash of "7.0 arcminutes", 82.3% efficiency, and static back-drive torque of 1.99 N m ([Zhu et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16591)).

### Failure modes

| Failure | Cause | How it is detected | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Winding overheating | Long holds, high ambient, blocked heat path | Temperature sensor or thermal model | Derate torque; warn the task layer to change posture or pause |
| Magnet demagnetization | Very high temperature or excessive current | Falling torque constant: more current needed for the same torque | Prevent through temperature and current limits; replace the motor |
| Bearing wear | Loads and impacts over life | Rising friction, noise, vibration | Service item; trend friction in diagnostics |
| Encoder fault | Cable, sensor, or magnet damage | Signal checks; disagreement between motor and joint encoders | Safe stop of the joint ([[2.3 - Motor Drives and Power Electronics\|2.3]] §2.3.5) |

## 2.4.6 Industry Progress

No commercial humanoid maker found publishes actuator torque, thermal or efficiency data. What is published comes from research groups, and is author-measured on their own hardware. All pages were accessed on 2026-11-22.

| Date | Platform | Claim | Evidence stage | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2025-03-20 (v2) | UCLA cycloidal quasi-direct-drive actuator, for legged robots | 10:1 cycloidal gearbox; continuous 37.5 N m, peak 89.9 N m (120 N m expected, limited by the driver); 1.40 kg; backlash about 7 arcmin | Research prototype, bench-tested | [Zhu et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16591) |
| 2025 | Berkeley Humanoid Lite | Drone motors with 3D-printed cycloidal gearboxes; gearbox efficiency about 90% over most conditions; actuator parts cost USD 188 (larger) and USD 136 (smaller) at US prices; whole robot under USD 5,000 | Research robot, open source | [Chi et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.17249) |
| 2025-06-14 | Humanoid knee with a variable-ratio linear actuator | Linear actuator and linkage with a "dynamically decreasing reduction ratio", designed for explosive output such as jumping | Research prototype on a humanoid | [Ma et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.12314) |

**What this shows.** Research actuators span a wide range: from a few hundred dollars of printed parts to a 1.4 kg actuator with 90 N m of measured peak torque. None of these reports comes with long-duration thermal or life data on a working humanoid.

## 2.4.7 Bottlenecks

- **Torque density against heat.** The joints that hold loads (shoulders) and carry the body (knees, hips) need high continuous torque in limb-sized packages without fans. Holding heat grows with torque squared (§2.4.2), so a small gain in required torque costs a large gain in heat.
- **Cost.** A humanoid needs one actuator per joint. Berkeley Humanoid Lite shows a low-cost route with modest performance; no commercial maker publishes actuator cost.
- **Magnet supply.** Brushless motors depend on rare-earth magnets. The Congressional Research Service reports that China "mines about 60% and processes and separates about 90% of global REEs" and "manufactures about 94% of REE-based magnets" ([CRS IF13171, 2026](https://www.everycrsreport.com/files/2026-03-04_IF13171_293b4f09964898af4a1178199bf89d4968a0feb1.html)). REEs are rare-earth elements. A humanoid industry at scale would need many magnets from a concentrated supply chain (5.6, Planned).

## 2.4.8 Implications for the Tote Transfer

- **The limiting joints.** In this example the knees limit the pick, needing more than this actuator's continuous rating in a deep crouch. The shoulders limit how long the tote can be held at reach (§2.4.4).
- **Holding posture.** Carrying the tote close to the body cuts shoulder heat by about five times against holding it at reach. Posture is the cheapest thermal design tool.
- **Pick height.** Raising the pick height, or letting the robot bend at the hips instead of the knees, cuts knee torque. That is a reason to set the tote's pick height in the operating domain ([[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]] §1.5.3).
- **Simpler alternative.** A wheeled mobile manipulator does not crouch on two legs, and its arm can be mounted at tote height. The knee problem in this example disappears.
