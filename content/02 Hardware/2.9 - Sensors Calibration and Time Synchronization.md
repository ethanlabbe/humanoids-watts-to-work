[[index|Home]]

**Scope.** This chapter covers:

- the sensors a humanoid carries, and what each one measures and how well;
- calibration, so that the sensors agree with each other and with the body;
- time synchronization, so that readings taken at different moments can be combined.

## 2.9.1 Purpose and Interfaces

Sensors are how the robot knows where things are, including its own body. A reading is only useful if three things are known about it: what it measured, where the sensor was on the body when it measured, and when. This chapter covers all three.

- **Energy:** small for passive sensors, more for lidar and for depth sensors that light the scene themselves. All of it sits inside the 300 W baseline of [[2.1 - Battery Packs Battery Management and Charging|2.1]] §2.1.4.
- **Data:** images and depth maps at video rates, IMU and joint readings many times faster, and touch and force signals in between. Cameras dominate the bandwidth ([[2.10 - Embedded Compute and Real-Time Communications|2.10]] §2.10.3).
- **Commands and feedback:** triggering, exposure and range settings in; readings, each with a timestamp, out.
- **Timing:** every reading carries the time it was taken, on a clock shared by the whole robot.
- **Physical:** mounting, the field of view each position gives, and mounts stiff enough to hold calibration through vibration, knocks and falls ([[2.6 - Mechanical Architecture and Structures|2.6]] §2.6.3).

## 2.9.2 Fundamentals

### Sensor types

| Sensor                                                  | What it measures                                           | Typical limits                                                                                                                  |
| ------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Colour camera                                           | Light from the scene, as an image                          | Needs light; blurs when moving; no depth from one camera alone                                                                  |
| Depth sensor (stereo, structured light, time-of-flight) | Distance to each point in view                             | Each type fails differently, set out in §2.9.3                                                                                  |
| Lidar                                                   | Distance along many laser beams, swept around the robot    | Sparse points; costly; reflective and transparent surfaces                                                                      |
| Inertial measurement unit (IMU)                         | Acceleration and rotation rate of the body                 | Bias that drifts, so position estimated from it alone wanders                                                                   |
| Joint encoder                                           | Angle of a motor or joint                                  | Resolution; gearbox backlash and twist if it sits on the motor side                                                             |
| Force/torque sensor                                     | Forces and torques at the wrist or ankle                   | Drift with temperature; overload in a fall                                                                                      |
| Touch sensor                                            | Contact, pressure and sometimes slip at fingers and palm   | Wears with use ([[2.7 - Hands Grippers and End Effectors\|2.7]] §2.7.2)                                                         |
| Motor current sensing                                   | Current in each motor phase, so joint torque by estimate   | Friction and gearbox losses blur the torque estimate                                                                            |
| Temperature sensor                                      | Temperature of windings, drives, battery cells and compute | Placed on a surface, not in the hottest spot; models fill the gap ([[2.8 - Thermal Management and Energy Budgets\|2.8]] §2.8.2) |

The table is this library's summary. Two rows are worth a note.

**Encoders.** An encoder can only report a joint's angle to the nearest step, and a small angle error at a joint becomes a larger position error at the hand, growing with the distance between them. Joints far from the hand, such as the shoulder, waist and hips, therefore matter most, and the errors of every joint in the chain add up at the hand. Noise and mounting errors add to the encoder's own resolution limit. If the encoder reads the motor rather than the joint, the gearbox's backlash and twist lie between it and the hand, which is why some joints carry a second encoder on the output side ([[2.4 - Motors and Actuator Selection|2.4]] §2.4.1).

**Temperature sensors.** Thermistors in windings, drive boards and battery cells feed the thermal protection of 2.8. A sensor can only report where it sits, so the hottest part of a winding is usually estimated, not measured. A motor's own winding resistance can serve as a thermometer, since copper's resistance rises with temperature ([[2.8 - Thermal Management and Energy Budgets|2.8]] §2.8.2).

### Error terms

Every sensor's error can be described with the same few terms.

- **Noise:** random scatter from one reading to the next. Averaging reduces it.
- **Bias:** a constant offset. Averaging does not remove it; calibration does.
- **Drift:** bias that changes slowly, with time or temperature. It has to be re-estimated while the robot runs.
- **Resolution:** the smallest change the sensor can report.
- **Range:** the span it can measure. Beyond it, readings saturate or vanish.

An IMU is the standard example of drift. Its rotation-rate bias, integrated over time, becomes an angle error that grows without limit, which is why its readings are fused with cameras, encoders and contact with the ground (3.5, Planned).

### Calibration

Calibration finds the numbers that turn raw readings into measurements in the robot's own frame.

- **Intrinsic calibration** finds a sensor's own parameters: for a camera, its focal length, optical centre and lens distortion.
- **Extrinsic calibration** finds where each sensor sits relative to the body: its position and orientation.
- **Hand-eye calibration** finds where the camera is relative to the hand or the robot's base, so that a point the camera sees can be reached by the hand. Enebuse et al. describe the two set-ups, a camera "in a fixed position with a fixed field of view (eye-to-hand configuration)" or "mounted on the hand of the robot (eye-in-hand configuration)", and note that the most common formulation "requires finding a solution to the homogeneous transform equation AX = XB" ([Enebuse et al., 2021](https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2021.3104514)). A humanoid's head camera is a third case: neither fixed nor on the hand, but carried on a moving body, so the whole chain from head to hand matters.

Calibration uses a known target, most often a checkerboard or a grid of circles, seen from many poses. Enebuse et al. call the checkerboard "the most common calibration pattern".

### Time synchronization

The robot moves while it senses. A camera image taken at one moment and a joint reading taken a few milliseconds later describe two different robots. To combine them, each reading needs a timestamp on a clock shared by every sensor and computer, and the timestamps have to be accurate to well under the time it takes the robot to move a meaningful distance (§2.9.4).

Two mechanisms do most of the work:

- **Hardware triggers** fire several sensors at the same instant from one signal, such as two cameras of a stereo pair.
- **Clock synchronization over the network.** The Precision Time Protocol, IEEE 1588, "addresses the clock synchronization requirements of measurement and control systems", where timing accuracies "are often in the sub-microsecond range" ([NIST](https://www.nist.gov/el/intelligent-systems-division-73500/introduction-ieee-1588)). Field buses used in robots offer the same: EtherCAT's distributed clocks synchronize each device's clock to a reference "with a precision of < 100 ns", correcting for the delay through each device and for each clock's drift ([Beckhoff](https://infosys.beckhoff.com/content/1033/ethercatsystem/2469118347.html)).

Synchronized clocks are only half of it. Each sensor must also stamp its reading with the time the measurement was actually taken, not the time it arrived at the computer, which can be milliseconds later.

## 2.9.3 Design Alternatives and Tradeoffs

### Depth sensing

| Method                | How it works                                    | Range                                                   | Sunlight                              | Failure Modes                                                                            | Cost            |
| --------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------- |
| Stereo                | Two cameras, matching features to triangulate   | Grows with camera spacing; accuracy falls with distance | Works: it uses the scene's own light  | Fails on plain, textureless surfaces and in low light                                    | Low             |
| Structured light      | Projects a pattern, triangulates its distortion | "normally up to 15 meters"                              | Does "not work under strong sunlight" | Pattern absorbed by dark objects, reflected by shiny ones, refracted by transparent ones | Low to moderate |
| Time-of-flight camera | Times light pulses returning to each pixel      | Short to moderate                                       | "struggles under strong sunlight"     | Mirrors and glass cause "multi-path" errors; dark materials leave holes                  | Moderate        |
| Lidar                 | Times laser pulses along swept beams            | Longest                                                 | Better than the cameras               | Shiny and transparent surfaces still cause errors                                        | Highest         |

The quoted entries and the camera rows' failure modes are from Zollhöfer's review of commodity depth sensors ([Zollhöfer, 2019](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.06835)); the lidar row and the cost column are this library's summary. Zollhöfer also notes two practical differences. Active sensors interfere when several share a scene, while "multiple devices do not interfere" with passive stereo. And time-of-flight cameras produce "flying pixels" at the edges of objects, depth values between foreground and background that exist in neither.

For indoor work at arm's length, as in the tote task, any of the cameras will do. Outdoors, or near sunlit windows, stereo and lidar hold up better.

### Calibration approach

| Approach | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Factory calibration only | Done once, with good targets and fixtures (5.7, Planned) | Lost when a knock or fall shifts a mount |
| Periodic recalibration in the field | Restores accuracy at intervals | Needs a target, downtime and a procedure |
| Continuous self-calibration | Re-estimates while working, from the scene or from the robot seeing its own hand | Harder software; can drift itself without a check against ground truth |

Enebuse et al. note that offline calibration does not suit applications where "frequent changes in setup and recalibration is an expensive operation". A humanoid that falls, is serviced, or swaps a hand is such a case.

### Where to sense force

| Option | Strength | Weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Torque sensor in every joint | Direct, accurate joint torques for force control | Cost, mass, a sensor per joint to fail |
| Force/torque sensors at wrists and ankles | Measures exactly what the contacts do ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map\|1.4]] §1.4.4) | Expensive, and exposed to impacts |
| Estimate from motor current | No extra hardware | Friction and gearbox losses blur the estimate; works best with backdrivable joints ([[2.5 - Transmissions and Joint Modules\|2.5]] §2.5.2) |

## 2.9.4 Sizing or Worked Example

All values are **illustrative estimates** unless marked otherwise.

### A 5 ms timestamp error

If a camera image is stamped 5 ms late, the robot pairs it with the wrong body pose. The error is the distance moved in that time: $e = v\, \Delta t$ for a sliding motion, or $e = \omega\, r\, \Delta t$ for a turn seen at distance $r$.

| Motion | Speed | Error from a 5 ms offset |
|---|---|---|
| Hand rising during the lift ([[2.4 - Motors and Actuator Selection\|2.4]] §2.4.4) | 0.4 m/s | 2.0 mm |
| Body walking | 1.0 m/s | 5.0 mm |
| Head turning, target 1 m away | 1.0 rad/s | 5.0 mm |
| Any of these, with clocks synchronized to 100 ns | 1.0 m/s | 0.0001 mm |

**What it shows.** A few milliseconds of timing error, which is easy to get from software timestamps, uses up nearly a whole share of the tote's error budget (below) whenever the robot is moving. Synchronized hardware clocks remove the problem entirely. In practice the robot can also look while it stands still, at the cost of cycle time.

### The placement error budget

[[1.5 - From Use Case to Engineering Requirements|1.5]] §1.5.5 splits placement error among three independent sources: perception, calibration and control. Independent errors add as the square root of the sum of their squares, so an equal split of the ±10 mm tote tolerance (T-3) gives each source

$$
\frac{10\ \text{mm}}{\sqrt{3}} = 5.8\ \text{mm}
$$

and the ±1 mm fixture tolerance gives 0.58 mm each.

The split need not be equal. An illustrative tote budget of 7 mm perception, 4 mm calibration and 5 mm control totals $\sqrt{7^2 + 4^2 + 5^2} = 9.5\ \text{mm}$, inside the 10 mm.

**What a calibration share means.** An orientation error in a camera's mount turns into a position error that grows with distance: $e = r \tan \theta$. At 1 m:

| Mount orientation error | Position error at 1 m | Against the shares |
|---|---|---|
| 0.5° | 8.7 mm | Over the whole tote tolerance by itself |
| 0.23° | 4.0 mm | The illustrative tote calibration share |
| 0.033° | 0.58 mm | One fixture share |

**What it shows.**

- **Angles dominate calibration.** A camera knocked half a degree out of place costs more than the tote can afford. A mount that keeps calibration is as important as the calibration itself.
- **The control share already has a claim on it.** The illustrative gearbox twist of [[2.6 - Mechanical Architecture and Structures|2.6]] §2.6.4 puts 1.7 mm at the hand under the tote's load, a third of a 5 mm control share before any control error at all.
- **The fixture case is ten times harder everywhere.** A 0.033° mount tolerance and sub-millimetre perception are precision-machine territory. Enebuse et al. note that "an accuracy level of 1 mm may be required for spot welding" on car lines, which is the fixture case's tolerance.

### Tote hook: how a pose error becomes a bad grasp

The hook of [[2.7 - Hands Grippers and End Effectors|2.7]] §2.7.4 has to land under the tote's lip. If perception, calibration and control together misplace the hand by more than the lip's depth, the hook catches the edge or misses. The wrist force sensor then reports no load where load was expected, and the robot backs off and tries again ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.5). Nothing breaks, but every retry costs seconds from the 60 s cycle, so pose errors show up as lost throughput long before they show up as failures.

## 2.9.5 Validation and Failure Modes

### Tests

| Test | What it shows | Pass criterion (example) |
|---|---|---|
| Calibration residuals | The error left after calibrating, from reprojecting known target points | Below the calibration share, with margin |
| Repeatability | Hand position when the same target is reached many times | Spread within the control share |
| Synchronization offset | Timestamps of one event, such as a flash, seen by every sensor | All within a stated bound, such as 1 ms |
| Operating-domain lighting | Depth and detection under the site's real light, including sun through windows and reflective totes | Detection and depth error within budget across the range |
| Calibration after a knock | Calibration before and after a fall or bump test | Within the share, or detected and flagged |

### Failure modes

| Failure | Cause | How it is detected | Response |
|---|---|---|---|
| Calibration drift | Knock, fall, mount creep, temperature | The robot seeing its own hand where the joints say it is not; growing grasp retries | Self-check against a target; recalibrate; service |
| Dirty or blocked lens | Dust, smears, packaging | Image sharpness or brightness checks; missing depth | Clean; fall back to other cameras; stop if blind |
| Glare and reflective objects | Sunlight, shiny or wet totes | Holes or outliers in depth; disagreement between sensors | Change viewpoint; slow down; ask for help |
| IMU bias drift | Temperature, age | Disagreement with cameras and ground contact | Re-estimate bias at standstill |
| Failed force/torque sensor | Overload in a fall, cable damage | Implausible or frozen readings | Fall back to current-based estimates; slow down; service |
| Clock loss | Network fault, failed reference clock | Timestamps jumping or out of order | Stop fast motion until clocks resynchronize |

## 2.9.6 Industry Progress

Makers publish which sensor types their robots carry, but not sensor models, accuracy or calibration practice.

| Date | Platform | Claim | Evidence stage | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Undated | Boston Dynamics Atlas | "Tactile and 360° camera view"; spec sheet: "Tactile fingers and palm" | Vendor-reported | [Boston Dynamics](https://bostondynamics.com/products/atlas/) |
| Undated | Unitree G1 | "Depth Camera+3D LiDAR"; joint encoder "Dual encoder" | Vendor-reported | [Unitree](https://www.unitree.com/g1) |
| 2026-07-09 | 1X NEO hand | Tactile sensing of "normal force, contact location, and shear" | Vendor-reported | [1X](https://1x.tech/discover/neos-hands) |

**What this shows.** The pattern is cameras all round, depth or lidar for geometry, touch in the hands, and encoders on both sides of the gearbox. No maker found states how its robots keep calibration in the field, which is the part that decides whether the error budget holds after months of work.

## 2.9.7 Bottlenecks

- **Calibration in the field.** Keeping a moving, falling, serviced body calibrated over months, without a person and a target each time.
- **Hard surfaces.** Shiny, transparent and dark objects defeat every depth method in some way (§2.9.3).
- **Touch sensors that last.** The soft skins touch needs wear out under industrial use ([[2.7 - Hands Grippers and End Effectors|2.7]] §2.7.7).

## 2.9.8 Implications for the Tote Transfer Example

- **The budget is generous.** At about 5.8 mm per source, ordinary cameras, a stiff mount and synchronized clocks meet it.
- **Timing and mounts are the real risks.** A 5 ms timestamp error while walking, or a camera knocked half a degree, each uses up a share on its own (§2.9.4).
- **Lighting still matters.** Wet or shiny totes and sun through dock doors can break depth sensing; test under the site's real light.
- **Simpler alternative.** A conveyor needs no perception at all, and a fixed robot cell calibrates its one camera once against a fixed table.

### Contrast: fixture loading

The fixture's ±1 mm tolerance leaves 0.58 mm per source, and its calibration share means a camera mount held to about 0.03° at 1 m. That is where most of the fixture case's difficulty lands. A fixed cell meets it with a camera bolted near the fixture and calibrated against it. A humanoid has to meet it with cameras on a moving head, through a chain of joints, which is why it needs either close-range sensing at the hand, touch to feel the part seat on its pins, or both (3.4 and 3.8, Planned).
