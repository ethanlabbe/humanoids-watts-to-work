**Scope.** This chapter covers the sensors a humanoid carries, what each one measures and how well, how the sensors are calibrated so their readings agree with each other and with the body, and how their timestamps are kept consistent. Perception software is in 3.4 and state estimation in 3.5.

**Target length.** About 3,000 words.

## Planned sections

### 2.9.1 Purpose and Interfaces

- **Energy:** sensor power draw.
- **Data:** what each sensor outputs, and at what rate and bandwidth.
- **Commands and feedback:** triggering, and exposure and range settings.
- **Timing:** timestamp accuracy and synchronization.
- **Physical:** mounting, the field of view each position gives, and how mounting keeps calibration.

### 2.9.2 Fundamentals

- **Sensor types:** each with what it measures and its typical limits:
  - colour cameras;
  - depth sensors (stereo, structured light, time-of-flight);
  - lidar;
  - inertial measurement units;
  - joint encoders;
  - force/torque sensors;
  - touch sensors;
  - motor current sensing.
- **Error terms:** noise, bias, drift, resolution, and range, explained once in general terms.
- **Calibration:** intrinsic calibration (a sensor's own parameters), extrinsic calibration (where it sits relative to the body), and hand-eye calibration (where the camera sees the hand against where the joints say it is).
- **Time synchronization:** why a reading is only useful with an accurate timestamp while the robot moves. Covers hardware triggers and network time protocols (PTP).

### 2.9.3 Design Alternatives and Tradeoffs

- **Depth sensing:** stereo, structured light, time-of-flight, and lidar, compared in a table on range, accuracy, behaviour in sunlight, behaviour on reflective surfaces, and cost.
- **Calibration approach:** factory calibration against calibration in the field or self-calibration.
- **Where to sense force:** joint torque sensing against wrist and ankle force/torque sensors against estimates from motor current.

### 2.9.4 Sizing or Worked Example

- **First example:** the pose error at the gripper caused by a 5 ms timestamp offset while the arm or body is moving at a stated speed.
- **Second example:** the placement error budget split among perception, calibration, and control, moved here from 1.5. The ±10 mm tote tolerance gives about 5.8 mm per source; the ±1 mm fixture tolerance gives about 0.58 mm.
- **Tote hook:** how a pose error leads to a bad grasp ([[1.4 - How a Humanoid Works - The Full System Map|1.4]] §1.4.5).

### 2.9.5 Validation and Failure Modes

- **Tests:** calibration residuals (the error left after calibrating), repeatability tests, measuring synchronization offset, and tests under the operating domain's lighting.
- **Failure modes:** calibration drift after knocks and falls, a dirty or blocked lens, glare and reflective objects, IMU bias drift, and a failed force sensor, each with its detection.

### 2.9.6 Industry Progress

- Published sensor suites on current platforms, dated and labelled.

### 2.9.7 Bottlenecks

- **Calibration in the field:** keeping calibration over months of use.
- **Hard surfaces:** reflective and transparent objects.
- **Touch sensors:** sensors that survive industrial wear.

### 2.9.8 Implications for the Tote Transfer

- **Error budget:** the tote's error budget is generous, but lighting and wet or reflective totes still matter.
- **Fixtures:** the fixture case's ten-times-tighter budget mostly lands here.
